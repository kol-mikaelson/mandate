## The Issue with the Delimitation Proposal and Its History

The core issue with delimitation reflects the wider fault lines within India's federal structure: immense socioeconomic disparity among states that makes formulating a single, uniform national policy nearly impossible. We have witnessed this tension across numerous issues, such as the anti-NEET protests in Tamil Nadu, contentious debates over central tax devolution to non-Hindi-speaking states, and even the subtle yet steady push toward Hindi imposition that has gained quiet acceptance over nearly fifteen years of BJP rule.

Fortunately, the Union government was not formed by a single party alone; the BJP requires the backing of coalition allies in both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha to enact any major delimitation proposal. Even so, allies like the TDP have signaled a willingness to accept the framework as presented. Such an outcome could prove detrimental to the long-term political representation and leverage of the southern states. By almost every developmental metric, the South has been remarkably efficient in curbing population growth through higher literacy, proactive family planning, and rapid urbanization, a demographic shift well-documented globally as a primary driver of declining birth rates.

The proposal tabled by the government sought to increase the total number of Lok Sabha seats from 543 to 850, linking this expansion directly to the implementation of the 33% reservation for women. This maneuver was clearly designed to put the opposition on the back foot: resist the delimitation framework and risk being labeled anti-women ahead of elections. However, cohesive and sharp messaging from the opposition prevented the ruling party from securing an easy political victory on the issue.

## Some of the Proposed Solutions by the Opposition

The Union government has repeatedly insisted that no state will lose a seat in the Lok Sabha. While technically true on paper, this assertion distorts the broader reality. Anyone with a basic grasp of mathematics can see that seat gains in populous northern states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar would heavily dwarf any marginal increases in states like Tamil Nadu or Kerala. Even without losing seats in absolute terms, southern states would effectively be reduced to an afterthought during national elections, as consolidating victories across the Hindi heartland alone would be nearly enough to guarantee a single largest party, if not a full majority, at the Centre.

As debates around delimitation drag on, several alternative models have been floated by regional and neutral parties alike. Some offer clearer paths forward than others, yet all face steep political hurdles at South Block:

* **Economic Output Weighting:** The Chief Minister of Telangana suggested factoring state GDP contributions into seat allocation formulas. However, this approach risks undermining the foundational democratic doctrine of universal adult suffrage by assigning disproportionate political influence to wealthier regions.
* **Proportional Seat Expansion:** The former Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu proposed keeping interstate seat ratios intact while expanding the total strength to 850. While this prevents penalizing demographically disciplined states and preserves regional balance, it remains a difficult sell to northern states, which would argue against capping their legitimate population ratios. It also finds little favor with the ruling BJP, which has struggled to secure consistent mandates across the South (Karnataka remaining the sole exception, and even there, lacking uninterrupted consecutive terms).
* **Institutional Operability:** Prominent Lok Sabha MP from Kerala, Shashi Tharoor, has raised practical concerns regarding legislative deliberation. He noted that even at its current strength of 543 members, the Lok Sabha struggles to allocate adequate floor time to lawmakers wishing to participate. Drawing comparisons to the Chinese Parliament, which represents a comparably massive population, he warned that an oversized assembly risks degrading into an unworkable rubber stamp, merely endorsing decisions engineered behind closed doors by smaller administrative bodies rather than facilitating real democratic debate.

## An Alternative Approach: The Case for Decentralization

Delimitation seems almost inevitable. Even in the unlikely event that Parliament executes another twenty-five-year freeze similar to the freeze initiated in 1976 and extended in 2001, such a pause merely kicks the can down the road. Postponement fails to address the underlying structural dilemma: profound, widening disparities across India’s major geographic regions, whether contrasting the Hindi heartland with the industrialized South, the commercial hubs of the West, or the distinct frontiers of the Northeast. Demographic projections by policy researchers like Milan Vaishnav show that under an unmitigated reapportionment, northern states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar could claim over one hundred and forty seats between them, while the collective voice of the South drops sharply in relative terms. Simply delaying the calculation allows demographic divergence to compound silently, making the eventual political reckoning far more volatile when the freeze inevitably expires.

Looking outward to other diverse democracies reveals proven frameworks for handling severe internal fractures. Belgium, for example, faced deep linguistic, economic, and regional divides that effectively split the country into distinct Flemish and Walloon communities. Rather than allowing demographic majoritarianism to provoke territorial breakdown, the Belgian state fundamentally restructured itself. It shifted from a unitary regime to an asymmetric federation, devolving significant legislative, fiscal, and cultural authority down to its regions and language communities. The lesson is straightforward: when demographic parity becomes impossible to preserve at the central level, federal survival depends on moving decisive power away from the center altogether.

A decisive push toward radical decentralization offers the only durable exit from India’s delimitation trap. In fact, this approach directly honors what the framers of the Constitution envisioned when they drafted Article 1, consciously designating India not as a unitary state, but as an indissoluble "Union of States." Modern political practice, however, has drifted toward excessive centralization, turning parliamentary seat tallies in New Delhi into an existential zero-sum contest. Recalibrating this balance across three core dimensions changes the equation entirely:

* **Preserving Universal Adult Suffrage and Democratic Equality:** Purely demographic reapportionment threatens to hollow out regional representation, while proposals based on state GDP output violate the bedrock principle of equal democratic value. Authentic decentralization sidesteps this paradox entirely. National parliamentary seats can reflect accurate per-capita numbers without sparking interstate panic, precisely because the central legislature would no longer dictate everyday regional matters. The democratic tenet of "one person, one vote" stays intact, while regional populations remain fully protected from majoritarian overreach.

* **Fostering Competitive and Responsive Governance:** Decentralization restores the principle of subsidiarity, the idea that governance is most effective when executed at the level closest to the citizen. States like Tamil Nadu, Kerala, or Maharashtra run on vastly different economic realities, social needs, and administrative capacities than Bihar or Uttar Pradesh. Empowering state capitals to design their own educational curriculums, industrial policies, public healthcare delivery, and local welfare programs yields faster execution, eliminates bureaucratic friction from central ministries, and ensures policies mirror real local demand rather than uniform central mandates.

* **Neutralizing the Existential Stakes of Parliamentary Apportionment:** Under the current, hyper-centralized structure, southern and western states fear delimitation because a concentrated ruling bloc in New Delhi can unilaterally impose language policies, alter fiscal tax sharing, and dictate provincial developmental priorities. When substantive taxing authority and policy-making power are decentralized, national seat distributions lose their life-or-death character. Losing a marginal share of seats in the Lok Sabha ceases to be a territorial threat when New Delhi’s reach over local matters is deliberately and legally curtailed.

This does not require rewriting the Constitution from scratch. The fundamental architecture is already present in the Seventh Schedule, which partitions governance across the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists. Over several decades, aggressive central incursions into the Concurrent List have systematically stripped states of their constitutional breathing room. Reclaiming that room means rolling back central encroachment, expanding state taxation capabilities, and giving state legislative assemblies primary authority over local developmental decisions. Rebalancing this division of powers addresses the actual structural fracture in Indian federalism, transforming delimitation from a looming constitutional crisis into a manageable administrative realignment.

> When state legislatures hold genuine sovereignty over local governance, seat distributions in New Delhi no longer pose a direct threat to regional self-determination.
